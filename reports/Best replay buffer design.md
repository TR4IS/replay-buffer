# Build a Replay Buffer Games Never Notice

The best replay buffer for this PC is a lean pipeline that runs outside the game, not an exotic one. It captures only the game's monitor with DXGI Desktop Duplication (DDA, the Windows API that hands an app a GPU copy of what a monitor shows) and converts each frame to the encoder's own colour format in a single GPU pass. NVENC (the RTX card's separate video-encoder block) then encodes it as H.264 at a constant-quality setting and a fixed frame rate. The encoded packets sit in a RAM ring the way OBS keeps them, audio is held as raw samples timestamped by Windows itself, and the MP4 is built only when you press save. Every other capture route is closed on this machine: NVIDIA's NvFBC is deprecated on Windows 10 and not licensed for GeForce cards, Windows.Graphics.Capture forces a yellow border on build 19044, and injecting code into the game is a ban risk under Vanguard and Ricochet. Published tests put hardware-encoded recorders such as ShadowPlay and Steam at roughly **2–6% average fps loss**. The prototype's **4%** is therefore typical rather than good. The target should be **under 1% with no change in 1% lows**, which no public benchmark we found has shown for any recorder. The prototype's loss most likely comes from three of its own choices: maximum GPU priority, an extra full-frame copy, and RGB input that makes NVENC borrow the game's shader cores to convert colour. Its ~100 ms audio offset most likely comes from the browser-based test and from audio and video clocks that start at different moments inside the ffmpeg pipes. Its HEVC clips fail because Windows 10 LTSC has no Microsoft Store and therefore no HEVC decoder, not because the encoder is wrong. Picture quality at CQ 19 is already near the ceiling, so the remaining quality work is correct colour tagging, a fixed frame rate that video editors accept, and sample-accurate sync.

## Every top recorder offloads encoding; they differ in capture and storage

All the leading recorders hand the heavy work to the GPU's fixed-function encoder. They differ in how they grab frames and where they keep the rolling buffer, and only OBS publishes its method. NVIDIA, Valve, Microsoft and Medal do not document their capture paths, so parts of this section are inferred from how the products behave.

**NVIDIA ShadowPlay / Instant Replay** historically used two private driver paths. NvFBC grabbed the whole frame buffer for full-screen games, and NvIFR read back a single window ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia_Share)). The current driver on this PC still installs `NvFBC64.dll` and `NvIFR64.dll` (local check), but NVIDIA does not document what the NVIDIA App uses today. Its buffer lives in temporary files on disk, on the OS drive by default ([Techgage](https://techgage.com/news/nvidia-adds-shadowplay-fine-tuning-options-in-latest-geforce-experience-release/)). In the NVIDIA App the save location can only be changed while Instant Replay is off ([Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/142202/change-shadowplay-save-file-location-nvidia-app?amphtml=1)). Adobe names ShadowPlay as a common source of variable-frame-rate video ([Adobe](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/faq-how-to-work-with-variable-frame-rate-vfr-media-in-premiere-pro/td-p/10348229)). GeForce Experience offered 10–50 Mbps ([Techgage](https://techgage.com/news/nvidia-adds-shadowplay-fine-tuning-options-in-latest-geforce-experience-release/)), the NVIDIA App added 120 fps capture ([PC Gamer via Yahoo](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/shadowplay-now-capture-games-120-141725194.html)), and GeForce Experience 3.8 added a separate microphone track ([NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-gb/geforce/news/gfecnt/geforce-experience-3-8-beta-multi-track-audio/)). One recent low-rigour comparison measured ShadowPlay at about **−2%** in Cyberpunk 2077 at 1080p, against **−8%** for OBS with the x264 software encoder, and reported no 1% lows ([tech-insider](https://tech-insider.org/obs-vs-shadowplay-vs-xbox-game-bar-2026/)). The largest documented "recorder" slowdown was not caused by recording at all: the NVIDIA App made games run **up to 15% slower** because of its Game Filters overlay ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/we-tested-nvidia-app-performance-problems-games-run-up-to-15-percent-slower-with-the-app)).

**OBS Studio** is the open reference design. Its Game Capture injects a signed hook DLL into the game, copies the frame each time the game presents one, and shares it with OBS as a GPU texture without a trip through system memory ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/win-capture/game-capture.c)). This only works under kernel anti-cheat because anti-cheat vendors allow-list OBS's signed files. When OBS 31.0 changed its code-signing certificate, Valorant capture broke until 31.1.0 shipped updated hook signatures for Vanguard titles ([linuxcompatible](https://www.linuxcompatible.org/story/obs-studio-3110-released); [OBS forum](https://obsproject.com/forum/threads/i-was-able-to-capture-valorant-gameplay-with-the-latest-obs-31-0-3.185315)). Its Display Capture uses DDA.

The OBS replay buffer is the part worth copying. It is a RAM queue of already-encoded packets, trimmed from the front one keyframe group at a time so that it always starts on a keyframe. It has both a time limit and a size limit, with defaults of 15 s and 500 MB. A save waits for the first packet stamped after the hotkey press, so the clip includes the frames that were still inside the encoder. OBS then streams the packets to a separate `obs-ffmpeg-mux.exe` that writes the MP4, so nothing runs in a subprocess until you save ([OBS source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/obsproject/obs-studio/master/plugins/obs-ffmpeg/obs-ffmpeg-mux.c)). OBS's NVENC defaults are preset P5, high-quality tuning, quarter-resolution multipass, adaptive quantization at strength 8 plus temporal AQ, two B-frames, and a constant-quality value of 20 ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/obs-nvenc/nvenc-properties.c)).

One OBS choice works directly against this project's goal. OBS raises its own GPU scheduling class to REALTIME, or HIGH when hardware-accelerated GPU scheduling (HAGS) is on ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/libobs-d3d11/d3d11-subsystem.cpp)). The 2019 commit that added this explains why: Windows deprioritised OBS behind full-screen games, "causing OBS to lag out whereas the games would still run fine" ([OBS commit history](https://git.tjdev.de/mirror/obs-studio/commits/commit/993c46c8a2e74f34454daf58fec58e1636e557c5/libobs-d3d11)). OBS protects the recording at the game's expense, and a recorder that puts the game first should do the opposite.

**Steam Game Recording** works through the Steam overlay and records only the game window ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/steam-deck-gets-a-built-in-game-recorder-120019196.html)). Its background buffer is an on-disk ring of fragmented-MP4 chunks, kept under a disk cap the user sets ([Steam Support](https://help.steampowered.com/faqs/view/23B7-49AD-4A28-9590)). Community tools join those chunks into an MP4 without re-encoding ([community gist](https://gist.github.com/safijari/afa41cb017eb2d0cadb20bf9fcfecc93)). The default "High" quality is about 12 Mbps ([Steam Support](https://help.steampowered.com/faqs/view/23B7-49AD-4A28-9590)). One creator measured CS2 dropping from 372 to 350 fps, about **−6%**, with background recording on ([bo3.gg](https://bo3.gg/news/thourcs2-conducted-tests-on-how-fps-drops-with-a-new-steam-feature)).

Among the others, **Sunshine**, the open-source game-streaming host, uses DDA on Windows, offers WGC only as a beta, and supports NvFBC only on Linux ([Sunshine docs](https://docs.lizardbyte.dev/projects/sunshine/latest/md_docs_2configuration.html)). Overwolf calls **Outplayed** "OBS based and hardware accelerated" ([Overwolf](https://go.overwolf.com/outplayed-tv/capture.html)). **Medal** documents only its separate game and voice tracks ([Medal](https://support.medal.tv/support/solutions/articles/48001220541-multi-track-recording)), and **Xbox Game Bar** records from the `bcastdvr.exe` service through an undocumented capture path ([WindowsReport](https://windowsreport.com/?p=90503)).

| Recorder | How it grabs frames | Where the rolling buffer lives | Saved file | Lesson for this project |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ShadowPlay / NVIDIA App | NvFBC and NvIFR historically; undocumented now | Temp files on disk | MP4, variable frame rate | Overlays, not encoding, caused the worst slowdown |
| OBS Studio | Signed in-game hook, or DDA | RAM queue of encoded packets | MP4 written at save by a helper process | Copy the buffer; reverse the GPU priority choice |
| Steam | Overlay, game window only | Fragmented-MP4 chunks on disk | Chunks joined into an MP4 | A disk ring works but wears the SSD |
| Sunshine | DDA (WGC beta) | Not applicable (streaming) | Not applicable | DDA is the proven Windows capture path |

The pattern is clear. Two storage designs are proven: a RAM ring of encoded packets (OBS) and a disk ring of segments (ShadowPlay, Steam). None of these tools runs a long-lived transcoding subprocess on the hot path, as the prototype does. None of the published performance tests we found for any of them reports 1% or 0.1% lows or input latency, so no recorder has publicly demonstrated "no noticeable impact" in the terms gamers care about.

## Ranked choices: what wins at each layer on this PC

Each option below is ranked against this PC's specific constraints: Windows 10 build 19044 without the Store, an RTX 4060 Ti with a single NVENC engine, kernel anti-cheat in the two main games, and Voicemeeter in the audio path.

### Capture: Desktop Duplication is the only clean option on build 19044

| Rank | Method | Verdict | Deciding fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | DXGI Desktop Duplication via `DuplicateOutput1` | Use | Windows API outside the game process; delivers GPU textures in the game's own format |
| 2 | Windows.Graphics.Capture (WGC) | Blocked | The yellow border can't be turned off before build 20348 |
| 3 | libobs plus OBS's signed hook | Possible, not worth it | Breaks whenever OBS's signatures change; little gain for monitor capture |
| 4 | Your own in-game hook | Reject | No anti-cheat allow-list; looks like a cheat loader |
| 5 | NvFBC | Reject | Deprecated on Windows 10 after 1803, not licensed for GeForce, needs a System32 DLL swap |

DDA wins on fit, not on raw speed. `DuplicateOutput1` can return a full-screen game's own back-buffer format, while the older `DuplicateOutput` always converts to 32-bit BGRA and "incurs a performance penalty" when the game uses another format ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi1_5/nf-dxgi1_5-idxgioutput5-duplicateoutput1)). DDA delivers at most one image per display update. Each frame carries `LastPresentTime`, a timestamp from Windows' high-resolution QPC clock, and `AccumulatedFrames`, which counts game frames that were merged because the reader fell behind ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi1_2/ns-dxgi1_2-dxgi_outdupl_frame_info)). The built-in cost is an operating-system copy of every desktop update into the duplication surface. Microsoft therefore says to release each frame just before acquiring the next ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi1_2/nf-dxgi1_2-idxgioutputduplication-releaseframe)).

The open question is whether DDA pushes a game out of *independent flip*. In that mode, Windows lets a flip-model game's frames go straight to the screen without the desktop compositor (DWM), "with the same efficiency as fullscreen exclusive" ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct3ddxgi/for-best-performance--use-dxgi-flip-model)). The only measurement found, on Windows 11 with an RTX 4080 and a 1080p60 borderless game, saw DDA keep the game in Hardware Independent Flip with unchanged latency and **4.1 ms GPU busy time both with and without capture** ([ritschy-mirror](https://github.com/gloomycoder/ritschy-mirror)). That is one game on one Windows 11 system at 60 fps, so it has to be re-checked on build 19044.

The alternatives each fail on a hard fact. WGC's `IsBorderRequired` switch only exists from build 20348 ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.graphics.capture.graphicscapturesession.isborderrequired)), and its frame-rate control only from 26100 ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.graphics.capture.graphicscapturesession.minupdateinterval)). NVIDIA says NvFBC "has been deprecated on Windows 10 and above", with build 1803 the last supported ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/capture-sdk)), and NVIDIA staff call it "a licensed product and not supported on GeForce" ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/nvfbc-on-geforce/54460)). The only workaround replaces driver DLLs in System32 and must be redone after every driver update ([keylase/nvidia-patch](https://github.com/keylase/nvidia-patch)), which is a needless risk next to two kernel anti-cheats. A home-made hook is worse. An OBS staff member has said OBS cannot bypass anti-cheat because "that would be considered malicious and harmful code" ([OBS forum](https://obsproject.com/forum/threads/game-capture-wont-hook-game-capture-is-just-a-black-screen.115678/)), and Ricochet's kernel driver specifically checks software that interacts with Call of Duty ([esports.gg](https://esports.gg/news/call-of-duty/call-of-duty-announce-their-new-anti-cheat-system-ricochet/)). DDA never opens, reads or injects into the game process, so it gives anti-cheat nothing to inspect. No Riot or Activision statement confirms this, though, so the safety argument rests on how the API works and on the absence of reported bans.

### Encoder: at recording bitrates the codec changes file size, not what you see

NVENC is a separate block of silicon from the shader cores the game uses. Several encoder features still borrow those shader cores (NVIDIA calls them CUDA cores): two-pass rate control, lookahead, all adaptive-quantization modes, weighted prediction, **encoding RGB input**, and the temporal filter ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)). So the preset (P1 fastest to P7 slowest) hardly touches the game. What touches it is that list of extras.

NVENC also has ample speed. One Ada NVENC engine encodes 1080p at these rates (fps, high-quality tuning, measured at RTX 4090 clocks; NVIDIA says speed scales "almost linearly" with clock):

| Codec | P1 | P3 | P5 | P7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H.264 | 885 | 647 | 283 | 211 |
| HEVC | 1037 | 706 | 411 | 181 |
| AV1 | 741 | 549 | 440 | 323 |

Source: [NVIDIA Application Note](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-application-note/index.html). The 4060 Ti has a single engine, because the 4070 Ti's AD104 is the smallest Ada chip with two ([NVIDIA Application Note](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-application-note/index.html); [OBS KB](https://obsproject.com/kb/advanced-nvenc-options)). At a constant 144 fps, H.264 P5 therefore keeps that engine about half busy at 4090 clocks. HEVC P7, at 181 fps, is uncomfortably close to the limit.

Codec choice matters less than its reputation suggests. NVIDIA measures Ada AV1 needing about **40% less bitrate than H.264** for equal quality at 1080p60, with the gap narrowing as bitrate rises ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-video-quality-and-performance-with-av1-and-nvidia-ada-lovelace-architecture)). VMAF is a 0–100 score built to track how people rate video quality. In one sweep on older hardware, H.264 NVENC reached **VMAF 99.5 at CQ 21.5**, and the tester visually preferred CQ 14–20 ([Gough's Tech Zone](https://goughlui.com/?p=48157)). At the CQ 18–19 this project uses, all three codecs sit near the point where encoding losses are invisible. The codec then decides file size, RAM use and playback compatibility, not what you see.

| Rank (default) | Codec | Size at equal quality | Plays on this PC with no setup | Why this rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | H.264 | Largest | Yes | Plays everywhere: Windows Media Player, WhatsApp, Discord, YouTube |
| 2 | HEVC Main10 | Middle | No (no Store decoder) | Smaller and 10-bit, plays in Discord desktop/web; needs VLC/mpv or a sideloaded decoder here |
| 3 | AV1 10-bit | Smallest (≈0.6× H.264) | No | Fastest slow preset on Ada, but the weakest sharing support |

NVIDIA's newer "Ultra High Quality" tuning is the wrong tool for gaming. It adds 12–19% efficiency, but uses **13–18% of shader time against 1–2% for normal HQ**, raises Ada encoder board power from 52.2 W to 72.5 W in one test, and adds 20–38 frames of latency ([arXiv 2605.01187 summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2605.01187)). OBS describes it as tuned for camera noise and advises against it for games ([OBS KB](https://obsproject.com/kb/advanced-nvenc-options)), and it needs SDK 12.2 while the prototype uses the 12.1 header ([NVIDIA SDK 12.2 notes](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/12.2/read-me/index.html)). NVIDIA's own recording advice includes lookahead and UHQ ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)), but that advice assumes the GPU is not also rendering a game. That assumption fails here.

### Buffer and container: keep packets in RAM, write the MP4 once

| Rank | Design | Trim granularity | Cost while gaming | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | RAM ring of encoded packets (OBS) | One keyframe group (~2 s) | A memory copy per packet | Lost if the app crashes, which is acceptable for a replay |
| 2 | Fragmented-MP4 ring on SSD (Steam) | One fragment | Constant disk writes | SSD wear, and twice the I/O on save |
| 3 | TS chunks through a long-running ffmpeg (prototype) | 10 s | A second process, pipes, a second timebase | Can't carry AV1; two processes to supervise |

MPEG-TS (the broadcast container the prototype streams internally) is a dead end. As of the 9.1 changelog, ffmpeg still cannot put AV1 into MPEG-TS ([FFmpeg Changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/master/Changelog)), and the muxer half of a 2026 patch was withdrawn because the specification is still a draft ([ffmpeg-devel](https://go.mail-archive.com/ffmpeg-devel@ffmpeg.org/msg187203.html)). The saved file should be a regular MP4 with its index (the `moov` box) at the front. A plain MP4 "is undecodable if it is not properly finished", and ffmpeg's `faststart` option moves the index forward with a whole second pass over the file ([FFmpeg docs](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-formats.html)). YouTube asks for the index at the front and no edit lists ([YouTube](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171?hl=en)), and Discord does not embed MKV ([Discord community](https://support.discord.com/hc/vi/community/posts/360052829453-MKV-support)). When saving from RAM, every packet size is already known, so a writer can put the index first in a single pass and skip the second one.

RAM cost is set by bitrate × length (our arithmetic, video only):

| Bitrate | 1 min | 5 min | 10 min |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 Mbps | 188 MB | 938 MB | 1.88 GB |
| 50 Mbps | 375 MB | 1.88 GB | 3.75 GB |
| 100 Mbps (the cap) | 750 MB | 3.75 GB | 7.5 GB |

A disk ring would trade that RAM for SSD wear. A 500 GB Samsung 860 EVO carries a 300 TBW warranty ([Samsung](https://semiconductor.samsung.com/us/consumer-storage/support/warranty/)). By our arithmetic, a 50 Mbps buffer running 24/7 writes about 197 TB a year and would use that up in about 18 months. At six hours of gaming a day it would take about six years, outlasting the five-year warranty. RAM first, with an optional disk overflow only while a game is running, is the right order.

### Timing: a fixed frame rate costs almost nothing and spares your editor

The prototype records *variable frame rate* (VFR): it only encodes frames that changed, each with its real timestamp. Players handle that fine, but editors do not. Adobe officially warns that VFR from "NVIDIA ShadowPlay … and OBS Studio" can play with audio out of sync in Premiere and recommends converting to constant frame rate ([Adobe](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/faq-how-to-work-with-variable-frame-rate-vfr-media-in-premiere-pro/td-p/10348229)). VFR can't be fixed at save time either, because copying an encoded frame does not repeat it. NVENC itself has no notion of real time: the frame-rate field only sizes the per-frame bit budget ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/dynamic-changing-of-frame-rate-in-nvenc/53068)).

The better default is a *constant frame rate* (CFR) grid. At every 1/144 s tick, the encoder takes the newest converted frame, and when nothing new has arrived it resubmits the previous one. A repeated frame costs only NVENC time and encodes to an almost empty packet, so it adds no shader work for the game to compete with. The grid snaps timestamps to within 6.9 ms (one 144 fps tick), which nobody can see. It also makes the bitrate cap per frame mean what it says, and it keeps a static screen from leaving frames stuck inside the encoder if B-frames are ever enabled. A 1/144 timebase, or 90 kHz (90000/144 = 625 exactly), keeps timestamps exact. VFR can stay as an advanced "smaller files" option.

### Audio: raw WASAPI with Windows' own timestamps beats cpal and ffmpeg pipes

| Layer | Rank 1 (use) | Rank 2 | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Desktop sound | Loopback of the default output (Voicemeeter AUX Input) plus a silent keep-alive | Capture of a Voicemeeter B-bus output | Loopback of the EarPods while Voicemeeter drives them |
| Mic | Hardware EarPods mic, opened shared | Voicemeeter B2 carrying only the mic strip | Exclusive-mode capture |
| Per-app tracks | Process loopback as an opt-in beta | Manual Voicemeeter routing | On by default |
| When to encode | Keep raw samples (PCM), encode AAC on save | Encode AAC continuously in-process | A continuous ffmpeg pipe |
| AAC encoder | FFmpeg's built-in `aac` | Media Foundation AAC (192 kbps max, 16-bit input) | libfdk_aac (can't be redistributed) |

Loopback recording copies what the Windows audio engine is playing. It only works on shared-mode streams, and event-driven loopback has been supported since Windows 10 1703 ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/coreaudio/loopback-recording)). Its catch is that it delivers nothing while nothing is playing. The standard fix, used by OBS for desktop audio, is to play a silent stream on the same device so the loopback never stops ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/win-wasapi/win-wasapi.cpp); [Audacity forum](https://forum.audacityteam.org/t/recording-no-longer-starts-until-sound-starts/46934)).

Per-app capture ("process loopback") is documented only for build 20348 and later ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/audioclientactivationparams/ns-audioclientactivationparams-audioclient_process_loopback_params)). OBS enables it from 19041 with the comment "MS says 20348, but process filtering seems to work earlier" ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/win-wasapi/plugin-main.cpp)). An OBS issue reports crackle and then drift 10–30 minutes in on Windows 10 ([OBS issue #8086](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/issues/8086)), so per-app tracks belong behind a "beta" switch.

Every Windows audio packet arrives with `u64QPCPosition`, the QPC time of its first sample, already in 100 ns units ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/audioclient/nf-audioclient-iaudiocaptureclient-getbuffer)). DDA's `LastPresentTime` uses the same clock but in raw ticks, so the two must be converted to one unit with `QueryPerformanceFrequency`. The capture thread should do nothing but copy. Microsoft warns that holding a packet longer than one audio period risks losing samples ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/audioclient/nf-audioclient-iaudiocaptureclient-getbuffer)). OBS uses event-driven capture with a large buffer and registers the thread with Windows' multimedia scheduler (MMCSS) as "Audio" ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/win-wasapi/win-wasapi.cpp); [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/procthread/multimedia-class-scheduler-service)).

Keeping raw samples until save beats encoding continuously. AAC works in 1024-sample frames of 21.33 ms at 48 kHz ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/medfound/aac-encoder)), so a continuously encoded stream can only be cut on those boundaries. Raw samples can be cut exactly at the video keyframe's timestamp, mixed into a share track, and corrected for clock drift at save time. They cost about 23 MB per minute per stereo source (our arithmetic). The trade-off is a burst of CPU work when you save, which the test protocol below measures.

For the encoder, FFmpeg's built-in `aac` is the default and can be shipped freely. libfdk_aac "is incompatible with GPL" and needs a non-free build ([FFmpeg docs](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-codecs.html)), and Windows' own Media Foundation AAC encoder tops out at 192 kbps and accepts only 16-bit input ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/medfound/aac-encoder)). The mixed track must come first and be marked as the default. FFmpeg's MP4 muxer groups all audio tracks as alternatives and enables only the default (or first) one ([FFmpeg movenc.c](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/libavformat/movenc.c)), and WhatsApp accepts a "single audio stream or no audio stream only" ([WhatsApp docs](https://developers.secure.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/messages/video-messages/)).

### Playback: Windows 10 LTSC plays only H.264 out of the box

LTSC has no Microsoft Store, so the Store-delivered HEVC and AV1 decoders are missing ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/a/1180572)). The HEVC package can be sideloaded with `Add-AppxPackage` ([Habr](https://habr.com/ru/sandbox/217109)), but it must be version **2.0.51121.0 or newer**, because older versions have a remote-code-execution flaw ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/a/1180572)). Even then, reports conflict on whether the legacy Windows Media Player uses it ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/a/12798237)), and one guide says WMP's free HEVC route won't play 10-bit ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/play-hevc-encoded-media-windows-media-player/)). Discord has played HEVC attachments on desktop and web since July 2024 ([Discord](https://discord.com/blog/discord-patch-notes-july-1-2024)), though some users still saw frozen HEVC clips months later ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/groups/SteamClientBeta/discussions/5/6400272691576721584)). WhatsApp's business documentation requires H.264 with AAC ([WhatsApp docs](https://developers.secure.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/messages/video-messages/)). The ranking is therefore H.264 + AAC in MP4 as the default, HEVC for viewing on this PC with VLC or mpv and for Discord, and AV1 for archiving.

## The prototype's symptoms trace back to fixable causes

**The 4% fps loss.** It was measured in a GPU-bound scene, the worst case, where any extra GPU work comes straight out of game frames. Four suspects stand out, in order of likelihood. The first is **maximum GPU priority**: the prototype calls `SetGPUThreadPriority(7)`, the highest value, which is the OBS-style "protect the recorder" setting, and OBS's own commit history says that at default priority Windows already lets the full-screen game win ([OBS commit history](https://git.tjdev.de/mirror/obs-studio/commits/commit/993c46c8a2e74f34454daf58fec58e1636e557c5/libobs-d3d11)). The second is **shader work it doesn't need**. Every captured frame is copied in full into a BGRA ring texture and then handed to NVENC as RGB, which NVENC converts to YUV (the brightness-plus-colour format encoders use) on CUDA cores ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)). By our arithmetic this path moves about 45–50 MB of GPU memory traffic per frame, roughly 6.5–7 GB/s at 144 fps, against about 30 MB per frame for the recommended path below. The third is **spatial AQ**, which moves bits toward the busy parts of the picture; it helps dark gradients but also runs on CUDA. The fourth is **thread contention**: NVIDIA warns that calling `AcquireNextFrame` on one thread while another calls `NvEncLockBitstream` on the same DirectX device "can lead to suboptimal or undefined behavior", and it prescribes asynchronous encode mode for this case ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)). An untested fifth suspect is forced desktop composition, which the PresentMon test below would expose.

**The ~100 ms A/V offset.** A browser test page can't isolate the recorder. Browsers delay their picture to match their own audio output latency, which the test page reported as 66 ms on this PC, while loopback captures the audio before that delay. The rest of the likely error is in the pipeline. Raw pipes into ffmpeg start their timestamps at zero whenever ffmpeg first reads them, so audio and video can begin from different origins. AAC's built-in encoder delay shifts audio by 21–43 ms in players that ignore edit lists. A capture callback that blocks on a pipe write delays the packet release, which loses samples ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/audioclient/nf-audioclient-iaudiocaptureclient-getbuffer)). A *constant* offset points to a mismatched start time. Mixing the two clock units would instead produce an offset that grows over the clip. The cure is one QPC timeline for everything and a self-generated flash-and-click test.

**HEVC won't play.** This is the missing Store decoder described above, not a fault in the encoder.

**Two misleading log lines.** The "keyed mutex was abandoned (0x887A0026)" message is Windows' stale text for **DXGI_ERROR_ACCESS_LOST** (local check on this PC). That error means the duplication was invalidated by a mode change, a desktop switch or a full-screen transition, and the app "must release the IDXGIOutputDuplication interface and create a new" one ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/direct3ddxgi/dxgi-error); [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi1_2/nf-dxgi1_2-idxgioutputduplication-acquirenextframe)). It is routine, not a bug. The "buffer underrun or overrun" lines come from cpal 0.18.2, which reports every `DATA_DISCONTINUITY` flag as an Xrun (local source; [cpal](https://github.com/RustAudio/cpal)). On a loopback stream that flag is normal whenever sound resumes after silence, and cpal hides the flags and device positions that would let the app tell a harmless gap from real sample loss.

## The recommended pipeline does one GPU pass per frame

```
Game → DWM → DDA surface (GPU, game's own format)
                │ one shader pass: colour → NV12/P010 (BT.709), cursor drawn in
                ▼
        small NV12 pool (4–8 frames) → NVENC H.264, fixed 144 fps grid
                                          │ encoded packets
WASAPI loopback + mic → raw sample rings  ▼
  (QPC-stamped)          │       RAM packet ring (trimmed per 2 s keyframe group)
                         └───── on save ──┘
                                   ▼
     cut at keyframe → mix → AAC → MP4 with index first → .part → rename
```

**Capture** uses one thread for the game's monitor only. The game's monitor is found with `MonitorFromWindow` on the foreground window, and the capture follows the window if it moves. Recording all three 1080p screens at 144 fps would triple every per-frame cost for footage nobody wants. The thread calls `DuplicateOutput1` with a list of BGRA8, R10G10B10A2 and RGBA16F, so Windows never converts formats behind the app's back, and it blocks in `AcquireNextFrame` with a 50–100 ms timeout instead of sleeping. Frames where `LastPresentTime` is 0 are cursor-only updates, so the thread updates the cursor state and skips the copy. For a real frame, one shader pass reads the duplication surface directly and writes an NV12 (8-bit) or P010 (10-bit) texture into a small pool registered with NVENC. The pass converts with BT.709 at limited range (the standard HD colour recipe players assume) and draws the cursor from DDA's pointer data. The thread then releases the frame at once.

This pass replaces both the old ring copy and NVENC's own RGB conversion. That fixes a colour problem too: FFmpeg developers found NVENC's built-in RGB converter ignores colour tags and produces BT.601 output ([FFmpeg commit](https://code.ffmpeg.org/Traneptora/FFmpeg/commit/b71069879010426ef8d7dce614086f8fb90eb0f3)), which looks slightly off in players that decode HD as BT.709. One forum user disputes this, so verify it with ffprobe on a current clip. The pool only needs a handful of 3 MB surfaces. Holding raw BGRA frames would cost about 1.2 GB of VRAM per second of history (our arithmetic). On `ACCESS_LOST`, the thread releases the duplication, re-enumerates outputs and recreates it. If another program already holds the system limit of four duplications, `DuplicateOutput1` returns `NOT_CURRENTLY_AVAILABLE` ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/dxgi1_5/nf-dxgi1_5-idxgioutput5-duplicateoutput1)); the app retries and shows a warning.

**Encoding** runs on its own thread in NVENC's asynchronous mode (`enableEncodeAsync = 1`, `doNotWait = 0`, `enableOutputInVidmem = 0`), as NVIDIA prescribes for screen-capture apps ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)). It follows the fixed 144 fps grid, starts a new keyframe every 2 s of time, and repeats the codec headers (SPS/PPS) at each keyframe so any group can be cut out cleanly. The GPU thread priority drops from 7 to 0 or lower; the test protocol picks the final value. The prototype's below-normal process priority stays.

**The buffer** stores packets in large 1–4 MB memory blocks with a small side index (timestamps, size, keyframe flag), so there are no millions of tiny allocations. Like OBS, it trims from the front one keyframe group at a time whenever either the time limit or a RAM budget is exceeded, and it never drops below two keyframes. A disk overflow (a pre-allocated fragmented-MP4 file overwritten in place) is optional, for long or high-bitrate buffers, and runs only while a game is running.

**Audio** drops cpal for raw WASAPI through the `windows` crate. The sources are loopback of the default output plus a silent keep-alive stream, and the EarPods mic opened in shared mode. Voicemeeter reportedly opens inputs shared by default ([VB-Audio forum](https://forum.vb-audio.com/viewtopic.php?p=474)), and Voicemeeter B2 is the fallback. Because the hardware-mic route bypasses Voicemeeter, a Voicemeeter mute does not silence the recording, and the settings screen should say so. Each source is event-driven, with a 1–2 s buffer and MMCSS "Audio", and feeds a lock-free ring of QPC-stamped chunks. A background thread builds a continuous timeline per source: it fills silence gaps, flags real glitches, and fits sample count against QPC over the last minute to measure clock drift.

**Saving** follows OBS. It waits for the first video packet stamped after the hotkey, takes a snapshot of the index, and cuts audio at the first keyframe's timestamp, resampling if drift exceeds about 20 ppm. It mixes desktop and mic into track 1 (default), keeps desktop and mic as tracks 2 and 3, and encodes AAC on a below-normal-priority worker. It writes `name.part` and renames it when finished. For version 1, use libavformat at save time only, in-process or as a small helper program like OBS's, because it already handles HEVC/AV1 headers, edit lists and files over 4 GiB. Later, a custom writer that puts the index first can halve the save I/O. The `muxide` crate exists but is young ([docs.rs](https://docs.rs/muxide)). On a SATA SSD, a 1-minute clip at 50 Mbps writes in under a second, and a 10-minute clip in about 8 s, doubled if `faststart` rewrites it (our arithmetic).

| Area | Prototype today | Recommended | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPU priority | `SetGPUThreadPriority(7)` | 0 or lower, chosen by A/B test | Let the game win, as Windows does by default |
| Frame path | Copy into a BGRA ring, ARGB into NVENC | One pass from the DDA surface into NV12/P010 | One fewer full-frame pass, no NVENC CUDA conversion, correct colour, 10-bit without an SDK upgrade |
| Capture call | DDA | `DuplicateOutput1`, blocking acquire with no sleeps, `LastPresentTime` stamps, cursor-only frames skipped | No hidden format conversion, exact timing |
| Frame timing | VFR | Fixed 144 fps grid, VFR optional | Editor-safe, predictable size, flushes the encoder |
| Hot-path muxing | Custom MPEG-TS → ffmpeg pipe | None; packets go straight into the RAM ring | No second process, AV1-ready, 2 s trim instead of 10 s |
| Audio | cpal → continuous ffmpeg AAC | Raw WASAPI, keep-alive, raw rings, AAC on save | Real flags, one clock, no blocking, exact cuts |
| Save | Remux TS → MP4 | Deferred save, one MP4 write | Includes the last frames, half the I/O |
| Default codec | HEVC, then H.264 | H.264; HEVC/AV1 opt-in | LTSC can't decode HEVC/AV1 |

## Three encoder profiles cover sharing, quality and archiving

| Setting | A: Share (default) | B: Quality | C: Archive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Codec | H.264 High, 8-bit 4:2:0 | HEVC Main10 | AV1 Main, 10-bit |
| Preset / tuning | P5, High Quality (P4 if NVENC is over ~70% busy) | P5, HQ (P7 is too close to 144 fps) | P6–P7, HQ |
| Rate control | VBR, average 0, target quality 18, max 100 Mbps | VBR, target quality ~20 (recalibrate), max 100 Mbps | VBR, target on the 0–63 scale, calibrate by VMAF or size |
| Adaptive quantization | Spatial on, strength 8; temporal off | Same | Same |
| B-frames | 0 at first; 2 later, not used as references | 3, middle frame as reference | 2–3, middle reference |
| Lookahead, multipass, temporal filter, UHQ | All off | All off | All off |
| Keyframes | Every 2 s (288 frames), closed, headers repeated | Same | Same |
| Input | NV12 from your own BT.709 pass | P010 from your own pass | P010 |
| Colour tags | 1/1/1 (BT.709), full range off | Same | Same, `colorRange` 0 |
| Plays on this PC | Yes, in WMP | VLC/mpv or sideloaded decoder | VLC/mpv |

*Target quality* (NVENC's constant-quality mode) holds visual quality steady and lets bitrate float, while the maximum bitrate caps the worst case at 750 MB per minute. Setting the average to 0 is how OBS configures this mode ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/obs-nvenc/nvenc.c)). CQ 18 sits inside the CQ 14–20 range a careful tester preferred and gives margin below the CQ 21.5 point where VMAF reached 99.5 ([Gough's Tech Zone](https://goughlui.com/?p=48157)). The same number does not mean the same quality across codecs, so HEVC and AV1 have to be recalibrated.

B-frames are frames predicted from both earlier and later frames. NVIDIA says using them as reference frames "improves subjective and objective encoded quality with no performance impact" ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)), and OBS defaults to two. They do complicate muxing: frames are stored in a different order from the one they are shown in, which can require an edit list. OBS also avoids B-frame references on H.264 because they "cause issues with some decoders" ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/obs-nvenc/nvenc-properties.c)). So Profile A ships without B-frames and enables two once the MP4 writer handles reordering. The size saving at these bitrates is real but unmeasured.

Profile B gets 10-bit, which reduces banding in skies and fog, without the SDK 12.2 upgrade, because OBS feeds P010 on older SDKs too ([OBS source](https://github.com/obsproject/obs-studio/blob/master/plugins/obs-nvenc/nvenc.c)). Feeding P010 directly also avoids the CUDA 8-to-10-bit conversion NVENC runs on Ada for HEVC ([NVIDIA Programming Guide](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-video-encoder-api-prog-guide/index.html)). NVIDIA puts the 10-bit gain at 3–4% efficiency ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/improving-video-quality-with-nvidia-video-codec-sdk-12-2-for-hevc/)).

Audio is the same for every profile: AAC-LC at 48 kHz, with the stereo mix at 256 kbps, the desktop track at 192–256 kbps and the mono mic at 96–128 kbps. These figures are our suggestion, since no listening test was found. Audio is a rounding error next to video either way.

## Proving "unnoticeable" takes interleaved PresentMon runs, not an fps counter

Average fps hides stutter, and published recorder tests rarely go further. The protocol below borrows reviewers' methods and adds statistics.

**Fix the system first.** Keep the driver, HAGS state, power plan and display mode identical across runs. Close the NVIDIA App overlay, Steam overlay and Game Bar so the recorder is the only variable. Note the game's PresentMode with the recorder off.

**Run the conditions.** Condition A is the recorder not running at all. Condition B is the recorder running with the buffer active. Start with an A-versus-A session to measure the noise floor, because no source documents typical PresentMon run-to-run variation. Then compare B variants with GPU priority 7, 0 and −3, AQ on and off, capture at 60 versus 144 fps, and HAGS on and off.

**Cover four scenarios.** Scenario (i) is the worst case, GPU-bound and uncapped, for example a Call of Duty benchmark at maximum settings. Scenario (ii) is the real setup: an fps cap below 144 with G-SYNC and Reflex on. Scenario (iii) is CPU-bound, such as Valorant at low settings and high fps. Scenario (iv) repeats scenario (i) with a save pressed every 30 s, to catch the CPU and disk burst of saving. If a game supports DLSS Frame Generation, add a fifth run with it on.

**Capture method.** Each run is 60–90 s on a repeatable route or benchmark, captured with `PresentMon --process_name game.exe --timed 60 --terminate_after_timed --output_file run.csv --v2_metrics --track_pc_latency` ([PresentMon README](https://github.com/GameTechDev/PresentMon/blob/main/README-ConsoleApplication.md)). Discard a warm-up run, then interleave A B B A A B so thermal and boost drift cancel out, with at least five runs per condition. Report the mean B − A difference with a 95% confidence interval.

| Metric (from PresentMon) | What it shows | Proposed pass threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Average fps | Overall cost | ≤ 1% loss, confidence interval touching 0 |
| 1% and 0.1% lows (99th / 99.9th percentile frame time) | Stutter | 1% low ≤ 2% loss; 0.1% low within the A-vs-A noise |
| MsBetweenDisplayChange spread | Pacing you see on a VRR screen | No increase beyond noise |
| MsGPUBusy | GPU time taken from the game | Report only; explains the fps result |
| PresentMode | Forced composition | Identical to condition A |
| MsPCLatency (Reflex games) | Added input lag | ≤ 0.5 ms |
| Frames over the cap (scenario ii) | Missed frames at your real cap | No change in p99 frame time |

These thresholds are proposed engineering targets, not industry standards. Reflex makes them stricter in a useful way: it keeps the render queue empty, so any GPU time the recorder takes shows up directly as lost fps and latency instead of hiding in a queue. TechPowerUp's measurements show how large GPU-saturation latency can get, with Reflex cutting Overwatch latency from 82.3 ms to 35.0 ms at Epic settings ([TechPowerUp](https://techpowerup.com/review/nvidia-reflex-review-test-ldat-v2/4.html)).

**Check the recorder too, in the same runs.** Log captured frames against expected frames, merged frames (`AccumulatedFrames` > 1), repeated grid frames, and NVENC busy percentage. A game-first recorder may drop its own frames under full GPU load, but it must log every drop.

**Sync and blind tests.** A built-in flash-and-click test should land audio and video **within one 144 fps frame (about 7 ms)** in the saved MP4, measured with ffprobe rather than a browser, the same flash-and-beep method reviewers use for capture latency ([Bright Side of News](https://brightsideofnews.com/blog/input-lag-testing-bsn/)). Finally, a blind test closes the case: someone else toggles the recorder between rounds, and you guess on or off over at least ten trials. Guessing no better than chance is what "no noticeable impact" means in practice.

## Conclusion

The best replay buffer is defined more by what it leaves out than by what it adds. Every large regression in the evidence came from something layered on top of capture and encoding: the NVIDIA App's filter overlay, OBS's REALTIME GPU priority, UHQ's shader appetite, injected hooks, and the prototype's own maximum priority and extra copy. NVENC sits at about half load even at 144 fps. Game impact is therefore a scheduling and memory-traffic problem, not an encoding problem, and the fixes are fewer full-frame passes, a lower GPU priority, and no shader-assisted encoder extras.

The quality ceiling on this PC is set at the edges, not in the encoder. Windows 10 LTSC can't decode HEVC or AV1, and video editors choke on variable frame rate. So the real payoff of newer codecs is RAM for longer buffers, not visibly better clips. Finally, the test protocol turns "no noticeable impact" from a marketing phrase into a falsifiable claim. If the prototype passes it, with interleaved runs, 1% lows, PresentMode and Reflex latency all inside the noise, it will have shown something no commercial recorder has published.
